From research papers to causal loop diagrams

I built a tool that reads a body of research papers and turns what they found into a causal loop diagram: a map of what changes what, which feedback loops connect the pieces, and where a decision maker could intervene. Every arrow on the maps below links back to the papers behind it.

The tool itself is not public. This page shows its results on two literatures so you can see what it produces and explore the maps.

How it works

PapersA review team's included studies, or a PubMed search screened by AI reviewers (two per record, a third breaking ties)
Read each paper's resultsEvery finding of the form "a change in X went with a change in Y", with its direction and the sentence it rests on
Check each findingReread against its sentence; drop what the text does not support. Code each paper's study design
Group variables into constructsHundreds of differently worded variables become a few hundred named constructs
Evidence mapArrows weighted by papers, flagged when findings disagree; a few reasoned links (dashed) so loops can close
Subsystems, loops and storiesA few diagrams around central constructs, with reinforcing and balancing loops
Intervention pointsLevers a decision maker can set, traced to outcomes: which way to push, what it helps, what it trades off

Everything runs on open-weight language models on a university computing cluster. The review team's own coding is never used to build a map; it is used only to test the tool.

Explore the maps

Pick a literature, then click any arrow to see its papers and every coded finding behind it in a sortable evidence table (paper, study design, cause and effect as coded, direction and the excerpt), click a construct for the variables it holds, or press Walk through to step through a subsystem one arrow at a time.

Limits

  • The maps summarize what studies reported, not causal proof. An arrow can rest on associations, and its strength shows the number of papers, not an effect size.
  • Dashed arrows are reasoned links, not findings; they are there so loops can close and should be read as hypotheses.
  • The GLP-1 maps have no human-coded benchmark yet; they were checked by spot-reading arrows against their papers (for the behaviors and patient-reported outcomes map, 7 of 10 sampled arrows fully right, 2 partly, 1 wrong).
  • Excerpts are short passages shown so each arrow can be checked; read the papers themselves before relying on any arrow.
