From research papers to causal loop diagrams

I built a tool that reads a body of research papers and turns what they found into a causal loop diagram: a map of what changes what, which feedback loops connect the pieces, and where a decision maker could intervene. Every arrow on the maps below links back to the papers behind it.

The tool itself is not public. This page shows its results on two literatures so you can see what it produces and explore the maps.

How it works

  1. 1
    Gather and screen the papers.A literature is collected (a review team's included studies, or a PubMed search screened by AI reviewers against written inclusion criteria, with two independent screeners per record and a third deciding disagreements).
  2. 2
    Read each paper's results.The results section, tables and figure captions are read for every finding of the form "a change in X went with a change in Y", with its direction and the sentence it rests on.
  3. 3
    Check each finding.A second pass rereads each finding against its sentence and drops those the text does not support. Each paper's study design (trial, quasi-experiment, cohort, cross-sectional, qualitative, model, and so on) is coded so arrows can show how strong their evidence is.
  4. 4
    Group variables into constructs.Hundreds of differently worded variables become a few hundred constructs (for example, many measures of quitting become "tobacco cessation"), keeping direction straight and keeping switches between products apart from use of a product.
  5. 5
    Build the evidence map.Findings become arrows between constructs, weighted by how many papers support them and flagged when findings point both ways. A small number of reasoned links that no study tested directly are added as dashed arrows, so feedback loops can close.
  6. 6
    Find the subsystems and tell their stories.The map is split into a few subsystems around central constructs, each drawn as its own diagram with its reinforcing and balancing loops and a short story.
  7. 7
    Find intervention points.Constructs a decision maker can set (policies, programs, prices, coverage) are traced along evidence paths to outcomes to show which way to push them, what that helps, and what it trades off.

Everything runs on open-weight language models on a university computing cluster. No step uses the review team's own coding; that coding is used only to test the tool.

How well it works so far

Tested against the human coders of the menthol and flavored tobacco review:

83%
of findings get the right direction, close to the 86 to 90% agreement between trained human coders
72%
of the findings human coders recorded are found and coded correctly from the papers alone
92%
of papers get an acceptable study design label

Explore the maps

Pick a literature, then click any arrow for its papers and their designs, click a construct for the variables it holds, or press Walk through to step through a subsystem one arrow at a time.

Limits

  • The maps summarize what studies reported, not causal proof. An arrow can rest on associations, and its strength shows the number of papers, not an effect size.
  • Dashed arrows are reasoned links, not findings; they are there so loops can close and should be read as hypotheses.
  • The GLP-1 map has no human-coded benchmark yet; it was checked by spot-reading arrows against their papers.
  • Paper lists show titles only. Read the papers themselves before relying on any arrow.
